# Foundations That Have Shaped My Research Journey

## Research as a Continuum

Looking back on this spring, I recently think that one of the most important things I have learned is that research does not exist as a collection of isolated achievements. New studies are grounded in previous knowledge, and even work that is later surpassed can remain meaningful because it becomes part of the foundation for what comes next. This perspective has helped me appreciate research less as a competition and more as a continuing process of building knowledge together.

I have also become more convinced that different academic fields do not need to be treated as rivals. They can support one another when their respective strengths are respected. In particular, I have become increasingly interested in the points where core AI methods can meet real-world scientific and medical problems without losing either methodological rigor or practical responsibility.

## Lessons of Medical AI and Translational Research

This spring, I continued to think carefully about the difference between developing AI methods and deploying them in real-world environments. I remain very positive about the potential of AI in medicine, but I also think actual clinical adoption requires careful verification, transparent evaluation, and evaluations from experts with medical knowledge. Research and real-world deployment should not be treated as the same stage of development.

I am grateful to Professor Jae Sung Lee as well for the advice, supervision, lessons, and encouragement that made much of this learning possible. I remember his guidance when I did not well understand the values of the direction of the research topics, such as federated learning, and how he encouraged me to think about why a research direction might matter even when its future value is not yet obvious. That experience became one of the important turning points for how I better understand research values. I am also grateful for the supervision on the head and neck cancer diagnostic assistant project, navigating various discussions and limitations, such as resource constraints, ethical concerns, and controversy about AI. Professor Jae Sung Lee's discussion about robotics in a lab meeting during my master's degree at FMISL was also one of the first reasons I became curious about robotics. Also, I learned that medical data can be highly difficult, heterogeneous, and very different from the organized benchmark datasets as well, which necessitating more of the benchmark datasets and evaluation metrics that can well reflects real-world medical environments.

## The Kind Research Environment

Another lessons that became clearer to me this spring is that a productive research environment should be transparent, constructive, and safe enough for people to ask questions, make mistakes, and improve. I have learned constructively discussing limitation makes research meaningless, making future work encouraged. I hope to become a researcher who can receive criticism without treating it as a personal attack, while also learning how to give criticism in a way that is useful, specific, and respectful.
